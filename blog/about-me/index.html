About My Research

How can economic development, a healthy environment, and human well-being support one another over the long term?

A company can become more profitable while the community around it struggles. A city can attract investment while making everyday life harder for some residents. Environmental improvements in one place can shift burdens elsewhere. These tensions lead me to a question that runs through my work: how can individual efforts create lasting, shared value?

I approach sustainability as a multi-level challenge, examining how firms, people, and cities can align their practices and policies to create lasting economic, environmental, and social value. Corporate decisions, public policy, and people's everyday experiences are deeply connected, yet they are often studied separately. My research brings these perspectives together through three themes.

01 · Places, policies, and connected industries

Companies do not pursue sustainability in isolation: their choices are shaped by local policies, regional resources, and the businesses they trade with. My research shows that local sustainability initiatives are linked to corporate practices, while a region's human and natural capital helps explain why similar environmental efforts bring different financial returns. Sustainability also travels through supply chains, with customers and suppliers influencing one another in ways that depend on their relationships and regional conditions. These connections raise further questions: how do transport and infrastructure reshape this process, and when does a cleaner business or region reflect genuine improvement rather than the transfer of environmental burdens elsewhere?

02 · What makes a sustainable transition worthwhile?

A better sustainability score does not necessarily tell us whether resources are being used well, services are improving, or environmental pressures are falling. My work connects environmental and social practices with efficiency and financial outcomes, showing that their relationships depend on what organizations do, what they disclose, and the conditions in which they operate. From companies to hospitals, this means considering economic viability alongside environmental performance and the value of the services provided. The same perspective opens questions about transport systems, energy technologies, and environmental markets: when we consider their full life cycle, who benefits, what does it cost, and where can meaningful improvements be made?

03 · Everyday environments and well-being

Sustainability ultimately matters through the lives people are able to lead. My research reveals widening concerns about emotional stress and shows how different combinations of workplace demands, autonomy, support, and leadership are linked to people's well-being. These findings point to the importance of looking beyond average outcomes to understand why the same environment can support some people while placing others under strain. Extending this perspective to cities, I ask how the places we live and the ways we move shape access to work, care, and everyday opportunities, and how urban and transport planning can support both lower environmental impacts and a better quality of life.

Looking ahead

My goal is to connect decisions made at different levels with the consequences people and places experience. A firm's success, a city's development, and an individual's well-being need not be competing stories, but understanding how they can reinforce one another requires looking across their boundaries.

I hope my research can help make those connections visible, opening possibilities for policies and practices that work better together. The question I want to keep exploring is what a sustainable and fulfilling future could look like when economic opportunity, environmental responsibility, and a good life are considered together.
