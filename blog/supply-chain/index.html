Supply Chains & Sustainability · Essay

How Sustainability Travels: Supply Chains, Industrial Convergence, and Human Capital

Sustainability practices can travel through supply chains, but their direction and reach depend on production networks and the human capital of the places they connect.

Corporate sustainability is often assessed one company at a time. Yet companies buy materials, sell products, and develop long-term relationships with other firms. These connections provide channels through which environmental, social, and governance (ESG) expectations and practices can spread. The question is how these influences accumulate, and why they produce different outcomes across industries and places.

Two recent studies approach this question at complementary levels. One examines the long-term environmental trajectories of country-industry units embedded in production networks. The other follows customer-supplier relationships to investigate the direction of ESG transmission and its association with human capital. Together, they show why greater connectivity does not automatically lead to a common sustainability outcome.

Industries converge within groups, not toward one common path

At the industry level, convergence means that groups of country-industry units follow similar long-run paths. It does not necessarily mean that every industry improves, or that all countries move toward the same standard. Xie, Ünal, et al. (2026) identify two environmental performance clubs among 126 country-industry units: 50 follow a relatively improving trajectory, while 76 follow a comparatively declining or stagnating one. Supply-chain structure is associated with these different trajectories. Stronger domestic upstream dependencies are linked to a greater likelihood of belonging to the improving club in Japan, but a lower likelihood in the United States; the corresponding relationship is not statistically significant in China. Export-oriented linkages do not show a significant association across the three focal economies. These differences suggest that production connections operate within distinct institutional settings. They do not establish that tighter supply chains universally improve sustainability. Comparable convergence patterns were not established for the social and governance dimensions.

Customers exert stronger influence on suppliers

The industry-level patterns raise a more immediate question: how do sustainability practices move between individual firms? Using a global panel of more than 18,000 firm-pair observations from 2014 to 2022, Xie, Okumoto, et al. (2026) examine customer-supplier relationships. They find an asymmetric pattern: customer-to-supplier transmission is generally stronger than the reverse. Evidence is especially consistent for social performance and the overall ESG score, while environmental transmission is more sensitive to the specification and context.

The relationship also develops over time. ESG gaps within established firm pairs narrow as relationships continue, providing evidence consistent with transmission rather than only the selection of already similar partners. Customer requirements and coordination offer plausible explanations for this pattern, but the results should not be read as proof that every purchasing relationship transfers the same practices. Firms' positions in the network matter, as do the environments in which they operate.

Human capital changes both the baseline and the connection

Human capital helps explain why transmission differs across places. The study uses a national human capital measure from the Inclusive Wealth framework, reflecting accumulated education, health, skills, and productive capacity. Higher human capital is associated with stronger baseline social and overall ESG performance, but can weaken the additional supplier-to-customer association, particularly in the social domain. One interpretation is that firms in stronger institutional environments rely less on upstream partners for sustainability guidance.

Differences between partners' home countries matter as well. When customers are based in higher-human-capital environments than their suppliers, customer-to-supplier transmission is stronger in the environmental and overall ESG domains. The corresponding gap effect is not significant for social performance. This distinction separates having a strong local foundation from the influence that arises across unequal contexts. It also explains why "more human capital means stronger transmission" is too simple a summary (Xie, Okumoto, et al., 2026).

Implications: connect standards with local capability

For firms, these findings support a more selective approach to supply-chain sustainability. Customers can be important sources of influence, but setting uniform requirements may not be enough. Training, knowledge sharing, and sustained supplier relationships are possible ways to support implementation where capabilities are constrained. Policymakers can likewise consider how education, health, workforce development, and coordination across production networks create conditions in which sustainability practices can spread.

These are practical implications, not interventions directly tested by the two studies. The evidence mainly concerns large listed firms, and human capital overlaps with broader economic development: supplementary analysis attributes much of the environmental gap effect to development differences. The common message is therefore conditional. Understanding sustainability diffusion requires attention to the structure of production, the direction of business relationships, and the capacities of the places those relationships connect.
