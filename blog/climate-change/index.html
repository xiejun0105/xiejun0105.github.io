Can Markets Drive Climate Action? Evidence from Mergers, Divestitures, and Disclosure

Markets can reward firms for managing climate risk, but financial resilience, changes in asset ownership, and more disclosure are not the same as verified emissions reductions.

Climate change becomes a business concern when environmental risks affect expected earnings, asset values, and financing costs. Financial materiality helps identify which environmental issues matter most to a particular industry. Xie et al. (2023) show that an industry-specific reassessment of environmental performance captures risks associated with stock-return premia. Across 34 countries, Shen et al. (2024) also find that environmental performance is associated with stronger financial outcomes, while distinguishing substantive performance from disclosure. However, materiality-based scores do not consistently outperform broader environmental scores, suggesting that market recognition remains uneven.

The next question is whether these financial signals change what companies actually do. Three studies examine different parts of this process: international oil and gas firms responding to climate policy through mergers and acquisitions (M&A), Japanese firms reorganizing their business portfolios, and Japanese firms changing the content of climate-related disclosures. Together, they provide evidence of market responses, while showing why a financial response should not be confused with a complete solution to climate change.

Climate policy changes the financial logic of M&A

For oil and gas firms, climate policy creates uncertainty about the future value of carbon-intensive assets. Chen et al. (2025) examine 1,147 listed oil and gas firms worldwide from 2000 to 2021, treating the Paris Agreement as a major policy turning point. Their analysis finds weaker financial performance following the agreement, with M&A associated with a mitigation of those adverse financial effects. The benefits are more pronounced for upstream firms and firms with stronger governance and sufficient cash flow.

Business behavior also changes: the study documents an increase in conglomerate M&A after the agreement, consistent with diversification as a response to climate-policy risk. This evidence points to financial adaptation, but does not establish that the deals were green acquisitions or that they reduced emissions. A company can become more resilient to climate policy without demonstrating a corresponding improvement in its climate impact.

Japanese firms respond through both green acquisitions and carbon-intensive divestitures

The Japanese evidence distinguishes the type of restructuring more explicitly. Yoshida et al. (2025) examine green business acquisitions and carbon-intensive business divestitures from 2013 to 2021. Firms facing higher carbon exposure are more likely to acquire green businesses or sell carbon-intensive operations. Outside directors are associated with a greater likelihood of divestiture, suggesting that governance helps translate climate-risk concerns into decisions about business portfolios.

The stock market does not respond equally to these two strategies. Selling carbon-intensive businesses is associated with significantly positive short-window abnormal returns, whereas the response to green acquisitions is not statistically significant. This is consistent with investors recognizing reduced exposure to carbon risk more clearly than the uncertain value of a green acquisition. It is not evidence that green acquisitions have no value. Nor does it establish that the sale of a carbon-intensive business eliminates its emissions: changes in ownership and economy-wide decarbonization are different outcomes.

Climate disclosure matters when its content is decision-useful

Markets also need information to assess whether firms are managing climate risk. Keeley et al. (2026) study approximately 1,700 Japanese firms listed on the Tokyo Stock Exchange Prime Market over 2019–2023, examining disclosures aligned with the Task Force on Climate-related Financial Disclosures (TCFD). Their analysis distinguishes narrative statements from quantitative information, including monetary estimates, quantified risks, and reported greenhouse gas emissions outcomes.

Using a multiple-period difference-in-differences approach, the study finds stronger financial effects for quantitative disclosures than for qualitative narratives, including higher profitability and a lower cost of equity. The timing and industry context matter: in climate-material industries, Scope 3 emissions-outcome disclosures are initially associated with lower profitability but later with higher valuation and lower financing costs. Scope 3 covers indirect emissions across a firm's value chain. These patterns are consistent with investors responding to concrete information, while recognizing that the financial benefits of transparency may take time.

The figure shows that disclosure became more common during the sample period, but reporting different types of information spread at very different rates. More reporting therefore does not necessarily mean more decision-useful information. The study evaluates disclosure adoption and content within Japan's policy setting, rather than isolating the effect of the entire TCFD policy regime. Its financial findings also do not, by themselves, establish that firms achieved additional emissions reductions.

Implications: judge market responses by what they change

The evidence suggests that markets respond to climate risk through several channels: the financial consequences of restructuring, investor reactions to business divestitures, and the value attached to more specific disclosure. Policy and governance shape these responses; they are not separate from the market mechanism. For managers, the implication is to connect portfolio decisions and climate communication with substantive operational choices. For investors, it is to distinguish risk reduction for the firm from improvements in environmental outcomes, and to examine whether disclosed information addresses the issues that are financially material to the business.

For policymakers, better disclosure and credible incentives can support this process, but success should be assessed beyond share prices and financing costs. Financial adaptation, changes in corporate behavior, and actual decarbonization need to be evaluated separately. These studies offer evidence for the first two, while leaving important questions about the third open. The practical challenge is to connect the market's response to climate risk with measurable progress in reducing it.
